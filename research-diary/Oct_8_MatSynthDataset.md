# Development Diary – October 8, 2026

**Focus: Machine Learning Dataset Evaluation and Material Classification**

Today was a shorter development day due to teaching responsibilities at Niagara College. Rather than making significant changes to the reconstruction pipeline, I focused on reviewing the training data used for the material classification component of the project.

Over the past week, I have been teaching machine learning and computer vision in my Digital Literacy course at Niagara College. One of the topics I emphasized to students was the importance of selecting appropriate training data, particularly how the quality, diversity, and characteristics of a dataset influence a model's performance when applied to new data.

This prompted me to reconsider my own approach to material classification.

### Reconsidering MINC-2500

My existing ResNet18 material classification model was trained using the MINC-2500 dataset, containing 23 material categories. The model previously achieved approximately 75.4% accuracy on its test dataset.

Although these results were encouraging, I had some reservations about how appropriate MINC was for my particular application.

MINC primarily contains material samples extracted from real-world photographs. These images often contain lighting variations, surrounding objects, perspective effects, and other contextual information. While useful for general material recognition, these characteristics do not necessarily reflect the appearance of the UV texture maps generated by my photogrammetry pipeline.

The system ultimately needs to classify surface textures extracted from reconstructed 3D meshes. These textures often lack the surrounding visual context present in traditional photographs.

I therefore decided to investigate whether a dataset specifically focused on material textures would provide more appropriate training data.

### Investigating Alternative Datasets

I reviewed several material recognition datasets, including:

- MatSynth: A collection of physically based rendering (PBR) materials and their associated texture maps.
- Dense Material Segmentation (DMS): A dataset containing real-world photographs with pixel-level material annotations.
- OpenSurfaces: A collection of photographs with annotated surface materials.
Of these, MatSynth appeared to be the most immediately relevant to my project.

Unlike MINC, MatSynth provides material texture maps, including base-colour, normal, roughness, and other PBR information. The base-colour images are particularly interesting because they more closely resemble the texture information extracted from UV maps.

I decided to use MatSynth to train a second ResNet18 classifier while retaining the original MINC model as a baseline.

### Downloading and Preparing MatSynth

I developed a Python script using the Hugging Face datasets library to download MatSynth's base-colour texture images.

To avoid storing the entire dataset, the script selected only the required image and classification fields, resized the images, and organized them into directories corresponding to their material categories.

The resulting dataset contains 14 material categories, including stone, wood, metal, concrete, plaster, ceramic, and fabric.

During downloading, I encountered intermittent pauses associated with streaming the dataset's Parquet files. The process also encountered an HTTP 404 error while attempting to access a file through Hugging Face's content delivery network.

I investigated the issue and revised the downloading approach to support processing individual dataset shards, retrying failed downloads, and preserving previously downloaded images.

The dataset download subsequently completed successfully during the early morning of October 9.

### Planning the Comparison

Rather than immediately combining the MINC and MatSynth datasets into a single training collection, I decided that maintaining two independent models would provide a more useful experimental comparison.

The proposed evaluation will compare:

1. The existing ResNet18 classifier trained using MINC-2500.
1. A second ResNet18 classifier trained using MatSynth.
1. An optional ensemble approach combining the predictions of both classifiers.
The motivation is to investigate whether training on material-focused texture maps produces better classification results when applied to photogrammetry-generated UV textures.

An additional consideration is the presence of baked lighting, shadows, reflections, and other artifacts in photogrammetry textures. Although MatSynth provides cleaner material imagery, MINC's photographic training samples may offer advantages when classifying textures affected by real-world lighting conditions.

Both models will therefore need to be evaluated against the same set of labelled UV texture samples, using compatible material categories, rather than comparing their independent dataset test accuracies directly.

I also considered combining the models using ensemble learning. This would allow their predictions to be compared or combined without retraining a single model on both datasets.

### Next Steps

The immediate priority is to train the MatSynth ResNet18 classifier and compare its results with the existing MINC model. I also intend to integrate model selection into the Three.js viewer so that classification results from different models can be loaded and compared visually.

Further work will include automated processing of reconstructed meshes, additional unit testing, and documenting the results for the final dissertation.

### Reflection

Although today involved relatively little direct development of the reconstruction system, revisiting the training dataset was a worthwhile exercise.

Teaching machine learning concepts over the past week encouraged me to critically examine decisions I had made earlier in the project. While the existing classifier demonstrated that material recognition was technically feasible, its performance on MINC did not necessarily establish its suitability for classifying reconstructed UV textures.

The decision to investigate MatSynth introduces an opportunity to evaluate how the choice of training data affects performance within the actual application.

This also reinforces an important aspect of applied machine learning: achieving reasonable accuracy on a benchmark dataset does not necessarily mean a model will perform equally well when deployed in a different environment.

With the submission deadline approaching, the remaining focus will be on validating the completed system, documenting its limitations, and presenting the experimental results.
