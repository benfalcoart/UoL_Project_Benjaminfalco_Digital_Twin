## July 14 to August 8, 2026: Research, Planning, and System Preparation

**Note:** This entry is post-dated and provides a generalized summary of work completed between July 14 and August 8. I did not begin consistently recording my work in a daily development journal until August 9. As a result, this entry summarizes the major activities and research undertaken during this period rather than providing a precise day-by-day account.

During this period, I was waiting for the results of my project proposal and feedback regarding the ethics review documentation. Rather than begin significant data collection or experimentation before receiving this feedback, I used the time to further research and prepare the technical and logistical requirements for the project.

A major focus was researching the hardware required to build the multi-camera image capture system proposed for the project. I investigated different Raspberry Pi configurations, USB cameras, camera mounting options, storage requirements, connectivity, and other components that would be needed to operate several cameras as a single portable system. I also began ordering some of the components once I was confident that they would be suitable for the project.

Power was an important part of this research. Since the capture system is intended to operate in locations where electrical outlets may not be available, I investigated portable power solutions for the Raspberry Pi and connected cameras. This included looking at battery capacity, expected power consumption, battery management systems, and methods of safely powering the system for extended periods in the field. I also considered how additional equipment, particularly lighting, could affect the overall power requirements.

I spent time researching existing machine learning work related to material classification. I looked for published research, existing approaches, and datasets that could potentially be used for identifying or classifying materials from captured imagery. The purpose of this research was to better understand what training data already exists and determine whether an existing dataset could be adapted to the project or whether additional data collection would eventually be required.

During this period, I also received feedback on my project proposal. One of the recommendations was to investigate **Gaussian Splatting** as a more recent approach to 3D reconstruction and scene representation. In response, I revisited some of the research and reconstruction approaches I had examined while preparing the proposal and began investigating Gaussian Splatting in greater detail. In particular, I considered whether it would be viable within the project's focus on a low-cost capture and reconstruction solution. This included considering its computational requirements, hardware requirements, available implementations, and how well it aligned with the goals and constraints already established for the project. This research was useful for determining whether Gaussian Splatting should be incorporated into the project or whether the existing photogrammetry-based approach remained more appropriate for the intended low-cost system.

Another area of research involved identifying suitable locations for collecting photogrammetry data. I investigated caves, historical structures, and other environments that could provide useful test cases for the reconstruction system. I started a spreadsheet for this information. For potential locations, I considered accessibility, lighting conditions, physical space, safety, ownership, and whether permission would be required to conduct research or use the equipment on site. This also led me to research the practical requirements of transporting and operating the capture system in these environments.

I also spoke with photographers I know to get practical advice regarding camera selection and the conditions I was likely to encounter. These conversations helped me consider factors beyond camera resolution alone, including low-light performance, exposure, consistency between cameras, lens characteristics, and the importance of controlled lighting for producing images suitable for photogrammetry.

Throughout this period, I continued refining the overall concept for the capture system based on what I learned. The research helped identify several practical constraints that were not immediately obvious during the proposal stage, particularly around power, lighting, camera placement, portability, and field operation.

By August 8, I had begun transitioning from general research into implementation preparation. Several components for the capture system had been selected or ordered, I had a clearer understanding of the power and camera requirements, and I had identified potential approaches for both material classification and field data collection. I had also revisited the reconstruction methodology in response to the proposal feedback and investigated whether newer approaches such as Gaussian Splatting were appropriate for the project's low-cost constraints. This preparation put me in a stronger position to begin building and testing the physical system as the project moved into its implementation stage.
